Most trading journals fail for the same reason most diaries fail as tools: they record events without a hypothesis. A list of trades with screenshots and a P&L column tells you what happened. It does not tell you what to change.

A journal that works has three properties: it is scored against a plan written before the trade, it separates process quality from outcome, and it is reviewed on a schedule you cannot skip.

Record the plan before the result is known

The critical design feature is timing. Any field entered after the trade closes is contaminated by the outcome. So the pre-trade block must be completed before entry, and it should contain:

  • The scenario and the level, in the same language your preparation routine uses.
  • The invalidation level and the stop distance, both as numbers.
  • The size, and the risk budget it was derived from.
  • What would make you not take the trade. Written down, because this is the field that stops most bad trades.

The post-trade block is short: did you follow the plan, what did the market actually do, and one sentence on what you would repeat or change. Deliberately brief, because a long post-trade form gets abandoned in a week.

Stop scoring yourself on money

The single most useful reframing in journaling is to grade the decision, not the result. A trade that followed the plan and lost is a good trade. A trade that broke the rules and won is a bad one — and it is more dangerous, because the win provides reinforcement for the behaviour that will eventually produce a large loss.

Reviewing this honestly requires accepting that a sample of twenty trades tells you almost nothing about your edge. It can tell you a lot about your process, because process violations are frequent enough to appear in small samples while edge only emerges from large ones.

Look for the pattern in the violations

After a few weeks, sort the journal by rule broken rather than by instrument or session. The categories are usually few and consistent:

  1. Entry without a written scenario. Usually boredom, or reacting to a fast move.
  2. Stop widened after entry. The clearest evidence that size was too large for the stop from the beginning.
  3. Size increased after losses. The behavioural signature of attempting to recover a loss rather than accepting it.
  4. Trading outside the planned window. Often a fatigue or attention problem rather than a trading problem.

One of these will dominate. Fixing the dominant one — usually by placing a hard platform limit rather than by resolving to try harder — produces a larger improvement in results than any change to entry technique.

Why this is psychology and not mysticism

The reason process discipline is so hard is well documented in behavioural research: losses and gains are not evaluated symmetrically, and the tendency to take risks in an attempt to avoid a realised loss is a central prediction of prospect theory. Work on loss aversion in traders suggests that this response is not fixed — that patterns of evaluation can be deliberately modified.

The journal is the mechanism for that modification. It is the only tool that shows you, in your own handwriting, the difference between what you intended and what you did. Nobody else's data can substitute for it.

Keep it small enough to sustain

A five-line journal completed every session beats a fifteen-field database completed twice and abandoned. Decide the minimum viable fields, commit to them for eight weeks, and let the review drive any expansion. If the journal has become a chore, it has already stopped working.
