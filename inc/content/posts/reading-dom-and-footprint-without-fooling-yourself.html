Order-flow tools are seductive because they appear to show intent. A large resting bid looks like a buyer who wants to be filled. An aggressive market sell looks like a seller who needs out. Sometimes that reading is right. Often it is an inference laid on top of data that does not support it.

What the data actually is

Depth of market shows resting, visible orders. A footprint chart shows executed volume at each price, usually split into bid-side and ask-side trades. Market-by-order feeds take this further and expose individual order-level detail, including queue position — but access to that depends on your data subscription and platform, and it is not what you see on a standard retail footprint chart.

Three things follow from that:

  • You are seeing the visible book, not the whole market. Iceberg orders, hidden and non-displayed liquidity, and order types that only reveal themselves on execution are invisible until they trade.
  • Resting size is a claim, not a commitment. A large bid can be filled, can be pulled, or can be placed to create the appearance of support. There is no way to tell the difference from the book alone.
  • Executed volume is fact. Trade that printed happened. This is the most reliable part of the toolkit, and the part most people under-use.

Four readings that are usually wrong

1. "Big bid = support"

Size on the bid is often absorbed. The informative event is not the size; it is what happens when price reaches it. If price trades into a large bid and holds, the bid is real for now. If price trades into it and continues through with little resistance, you have just watched it fail — which is itself useful information, and frequently a better trade than the one you were planning.

2. "Aggressive selling = bearish"

Aggressive market selling into a level that does not move is absorption. Someone is taking the other side of every market order and price is not going down. Read in isolation, a delta number is noise; read against the price response it produced, it is a clue.

3. "The chart shows trapped traders"

Trap narratives attach a story to a price event you cannot verify. You know price failed to hold a level. You do not know who is trapped, how much size they hold, or whether they are hedging something larger. Trade the observable, not the story.

4. "This is institutional"

Attributing individual prints to "institutions" is unfalsifiable. What you can say is that a specific volume pattern executed at a specific price and that price did or did not subsequently move. Everything else is decoration.

A defensible workflow

  1. Mark levels first, without order flow. Prior extremes, prior value edges, overnight range. If a level is not on the list, order flow cannot put it there.
  2. Wait for interaction. When price arrives at a level, watch the response: does volume increase and price stop, or does price cut through?
  3. Score the response in words, not charts. "Bid absorbed three minutes of selling without losing the level" is a testable observation. "Strong support" is not.
  4. Write the invalidation before entry. If the level fails and price accepts below it, the read is dead. No averaging, no reinterpreting.

Practical settings note

Footprint charts are only as good as their aggregation. A five-tick footprint on an illiquid contract produces noise; a large tick-per-row setting on a fast market hides the detail you wanted. Because liquidity varies by instrument and session, calibrate per contract rather than using one global setting — CME's own work on assessing liquidity with book depth is a useful reminder that depth alone is an incomplete proxy.
