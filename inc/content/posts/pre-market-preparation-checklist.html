The purpose of pre-market preparation is not to predict the day. It is to arrive with a small set of scenarios already written down, so that the opening minutes test a plan rather than produce a reaction.

Done properly this takes fifteen minutes. Done badly it consumes an hour and produces nothing actionable, usually because it has drifted into reading opinions about the market instead of describing the market.

The fifteen minutes, in order

Minutes 1–3: context

What happened overnight? Record the overnight range, the current position relative to it, and whether the session is opening inside or outside the prior day's range. Note the overnight high and low as specific numbers. Two facts and two prices.

If a large overnight gap exists, note it as a condition rather than a signal. Gaps are a statement about what changed while you were not trading, not a direction.

Minutes 4–7: levels

Write down no more than four levels. A useful filter is to ask of each candidate: if price reaches this, will I change what I am doing? If the answer is no, it does not belong on the list.

The standard candidates are prior session high and low, prior value area edges and point of control, the overnight range extremes, and any level where price clearly failed to accept in the last few sessions. Everything else — moving averages, pivots, Fibonacci retracements of the wrong swing — is a candidate for exclusion.

Minutes 8–12: scenarios

Write three scenarios, each with the same three components. This structure forces you to define failure, which is the part that is usually skipped.

  1. If price opens and holds above X: the auction is exploring upward; my interest is in retests of X as support; the read is wrong if price accepts back below X.
  2. If price opens and holds below Y: the mirror case.
  3. If price is trapped between X and Y: the auction is balanced; my interest is fading the extremes with tight size, or doing nothing. On many days this is the correct answer and the disciplined outcome is to trade nothing.

Minutes 13–15: constraints

Before the open, fix the numbers that are not negotiable:

  • Maximum risk for the day, in currency, with the order placed if your platform supports it.
  • Maximum number of trades. A number, chosen in advance, not after three losses.
  • Events on the calendar that could produce a liquidity gap, and whether you intend to be flat through them.
  • Size for scenario one. If the answer differs between scenarios, you have not finished the arithmetic.

What to leave out

Do not read other people's directional calls during this window. The output of the routine is a set of your scenarios tied to your invalidation levels. Borrowed conviction is the fastest way to end up in a position you cannot explain.

Do not look at a watchlist of twenty instruments. Depth on three or four beats shallow attention on twenty. If you trade index futures, the whole routine can be completed on one contract and one higher-timeframe chart.

When the routine is over

When the fifteen minutes expire, preparation is finished. That is the point of a fixed time budget: it prevents the pre-market period from becoming an anxiety-generating activity that substitutes for having a plan.

Write the plan where you will see it during the session — on the chart, in a notepad, on paper. A plan that only exists in your head is not a plan; it is an intention that will be revised by the first uncomfortable price tick.

Review the plan, not just the trades

At the end of the session, score the plan rather than the outcome. How many of your three scenarios were actually observed? Did you trade outside them? Did the levels you excluded turn out to matter? Over a few weeks, this scoring tells you whether the routine is producing useful context or just consuming time — which is the only honest way to improve it.
