Ask a losing trader what went wrong and the answer is usually about direction. Look at the account statement and the answer is usually about size. A position that is three times too large converts an ordinary losing trade into an account-threatening event, and it does so without any change in the quality of the idea.

The order of operations

Sizing is not a decision you make after the entry. It is arithmetic performed in a fixed sequence:

  1. Define the invalidation level. Where is the idea wrong? This is a market-structure question, not a comfort question.
  2. Measure the stop distance in ticks. Entry to invalidation, using the instrument's tick size. For the Micro E-mini S&P 500, for example, contract specifications define the tick size and value precisely — always confirm against the exchange specification rather than a forum post.
  3. Choose a risk budget per trade. A percentage of account equity you are willing to lose if the stop is hit. This is a policy decision, and it should be small enough that a string of losses does not threaten the account.
  4. Divide. Contracts = risk budget ÷ (stop distance in ticks × tick value). Round down, not to the nearest.

Run in this order, a wider stop automatically produces a smaller position. That is what makes structural stop placement affordable, and it is the mechanism most traders skip.

Why "risk 1%" is not a complete policy

A per-trade percentage is necessary and insufficient. Two positions at one percent each can be a single two-percent position if they express the same view in correlated instruments. Four index futures long positions — ES, NQ, YM, RTY — are one macro bet at four times the size, not four independent trades.

The practical addition is an aggregate exposure check before entry: what is my total risk if everything I am holding moves against me at the same time? If you cannot answer that in one number, your risk is not being managed, it is being accumulated.

What the Kelly criterion actually says

The Kelly criterion gives the bet size that maximises the long-run growth rate of capital, given a known edge and known odds. It is genuinely useful — Ed Thorp's work applying it to blackjack and then to markets is the canonical treatment — but it comes with two properties that make naive use dangerous for traders.

First, it requires accurate estimates of your edge and your payoff distribution. Traders consistently overestimate both. Second, full Kelly produces drawdowns that most people cannot psychologically survive even when the mathematics is correct; the growth-optimal bet is not the comfort-optimal bet.

The common compromise is fractional Kelly — a fraction of the full Kelly stake, frequently a quarter or less — which gives up some theoretical growth in exchange for materially smaller drawdowns. For most discretionary traders, the honest framing is that Kelly is a disciplined way to think about size relative to edge, not a formula to be applied literally to a subjective probability.

Daily and weekly circuit breakers

Sizing controls the damage of one trade. It does not control what happens after four losses in a morning. That requires a second layer:

  • Daily loss limit. A hard number, ideally enforced at the broker so it does not depend on your discipline mid-session. When it is hit, the trading day is over.
  • Consecutive-loss rule. A stop after N losses in a row regardless of size, because sequences of losses correlate with poor conditions and deteriorating judgement.
  • Weekly review gate. A short written review before the next week begins. Not optional, and not skipped when the week went well.

Backtest the sequence, not the trade

Individual trade outcomes tell you very little. A run of ten losses inside a hundred trades is statistically unremarkable and will happen to real strategies. The reason sizing deserves this much attention is that it determines whether a normal, expected losing sequence is survivable. If a routine streak ends your account, no edge is large enough to matter.
